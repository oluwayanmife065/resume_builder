Personal Pitch & Technical Perspective:

I’m a Computer Science and Economics double major at Hamilton College, originally from Nigeria. I was first introduced to both fields at the college level, which made the transition challenging at first, but it also helped me develop strong problem-solving skills and adaptability. Over time, I became really interested in how technology and economic thinking can work together, especially to improve financial literacy and expand economic opportunities in developing countries.

To build toward that, I’ve gained experience across both research and software engineering. In economics research, I worked extensively with data analysis, STM topic modeling, and LLMs over real-world legal datasets. In software engineering internships, I worked across the full stack—building backend APIs in C#, integrating them with React frontends, and writing unit tests, all within collaborative workflows on Azure DevOps.

One project I’m particularly proud of is Sesame Exchange, a Flutter web application I built using Dart, Firebase, and Cloudinary. It’s a campus marketplace that uses AI to automatically generate descriptions from uploaded images, making it easier and safer for students to post items. More recently, I’ve been developing a stock analysis platform using Python, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, and React, where I’m experimenting with LSTM and transformer models to explore stock price trends and investment insights.

Looking ahead, I’m interested in roles where I can combine software engineering, data, and finance to build tools that help people make better financial decisions. That’s why I’m especially excited about companies where I can work on data-driven financial systems at scale. Long term, I want to build technology that improves financial access and decision-making, particularly for people in developing economies.

On Technical Philosophy & Python:
I appreciate Python for its rapid prototyping, versatile library ecosystem, and powerful built-in algorithms (like Timsort). At the same time, I recognize its trade-offs: lack of granular memory control and higher abstraction compared to C++ or C#. When debugging production latency and reliability issues—like hallucination in local RAG pipelines—I rely on deterministic evaluations: cosine similarity thresholds against ingested chunks and safety filters with Llama Guard. In team collaboration, when debating technical approaches, I prioritize data: measuring precision, recall, and accuracy across test sets rather than relying on intuition.
